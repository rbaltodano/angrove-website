Starting a Conversation

A conversation is where you ask Angrove a question and talk it through. Tap New Conversation on Home or at the bottom of the menu to begin. Think of it as a patient study partner: you can ask anything, push back, and keep going.

What it does

Angrove answers your question in plain prose, and you can keep the discussion going with follow-ups for as long as you like.

How to use it

Type your question and send it. Beneath each answer, Copy copies the text and Regenerate asks for a fresh answer. Follow up by asking for a clarification, an example, or an objection. Type /rename to name the conversation you’re in, or /rename followed by a name to set it immediately. In a very long conversation, type /compact to summarize older parts so Angrove can keep going (you still see everything), or /clear to start the conversation over.

When it helps

Ask “How does habit shape character?”, then ask for an everyday example. Answers can make mistakes, so check important claims against their sources.

Definitions & Saved Insights

When Angrove uses a word that matters, it underlines it. Tap it and you get a short explanation of what that word means in your conversation, not a dictionary entry. If it’s worth keeping, save it. A saved definition is called an Insight, and your Insights become the building blocks of everything else in the app: the Insight Tree, Study, and Midpoints.

Tap an underlined word to open its definition, then tap Save to add it to your Insights.

What it does

An Insight is a definition you chose to keep. It explains a term in the context of the discussion where you found it, so it still makes sense when you come back to it later.

How to use it

Tap an underlined term in an answer. When its definition opens, tap Save to keep it, or Ask to ask about it in your next question. Saved Insights appear in Insights in the menu and in the conversation’s tree. Saving the same word from a different discussion adds its new meaning to the same card.

When it helps

Save “grace” while discussing theology, then return to it later without searching through the whole conversation.

Understanding the Insight Tree

Every conversation has an Insight Tree: a map of the ideas you’ve explored. Open it with Insight Tree at the top of a conversation, or by swiping left. It shows how your saved Insights relate to one another, so you can see the shape of a subject instead of scrolling back through a long discussion.

Any Insights you saved in the example above join this tree, which starts with Cynicism and Epicureanism.

What it does

The tree gathers your Insights around broader subjects called Node Concepts. Angrove adds a Node Concept when an answer opens up a new subject; Insights are only ever added by you.

How to use it

Drag to move around, pinch to zoom, and tap anything to read its card. Tap Ask in the model controls to ask a question about the hovered Insight or Node Concept. Each Node Concept hangs from the subject it’s closest to, and shorter lines mean the ideas are closer in meaning. Fainter lines link a Midpoint to its other sources. Insights in the menu has a larger tree that brings together everything you’ve saved; accept its update prompt to add your latest bookmarks. The number beside Insights in the menu counts saved Insights you haven’t seen there yet.

When it helps

Use the map to spot how several definitions belong to one larger question. Distance shows how related two ideas are, not whether they’re true or important.

Meaning as numbers

Each saved concept is represented by an embedding vector: 384 numbers generated by the existing all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model, a small transformer that runs on your phone through Core ML. For an Insight, the model reads its title and definition together, so it can represent meaning in context rather than just matching words. “Mercy” and “forgiveness,” for example, can point in similar semantic directions.

The 384 dimensions are not 384 individual meanings. Semantic information is distributed across the vector’s components, whose combinations allow effectively infinitely many possible directions. Think of the vector as a whole, rather than a checklist with one meaning per number.

Cosine similarity

Think of each concept as an arrow in 384-dimensional space. Cosine similarity measures how closely two arrows’ directions align. Similar directions suggest related meanings; the arrows’ lengths, or magnitudes, are not the important signal:

sim(a, b) = (a · b) / (‖a‖ ‖b‖)     d(a, b) = 1 − sim(a, b)

The dot product a · b measures alignment, and dividing by both magnitudes removes the effect of length. Arrows pointing the same way score 1; perpendicular arrows score 0; exactly opposite arrows score −1. A score is a measure of relatedness in this model’s space, not a probability or a judgment of truth.

Angrove converts similarity into semantic distance with d = 1 − sim: a higher similarity becomes a smaller distance. These distances guide clustering and visualization. Cosine distance is a useful dissimilarity measure, though it does not satisfy every property of a geometric metric.

Building the tree

  • 1Seed. After each answer, Gemma names the subject of that turn with a short label and summary. Angrove embeds “label. summary”, and the subject becomes a new Node Concept only if its similarity to every existing subject is below 0.60, so ordinary follow-ups don’t create duplicates.
  • 2Attach. A saved Insight is compared with each Node Concept, using the higher of its similarity to the Node’s members (their mean embedding) and to the Node’s subject. It joins the best match at 0.35 or above; otherwise it starts a Node of its own. At 0.50 or above it is a close member; between the two it hangs loosely. Membership is sticky: once placed, an Insight keeps its Node as others come and go.
  • 3Bud. When three or more of a Node’s loose Insights belong together (each at least 0.55 similar to the group’s own center, and closer to one another than to their Node), they bud off into a new Node Concept that hangs from the original. If they fit another existing Node better, they move there instead, so the same subject never appears twice.
  • 4Merge. Node Concepts found separately fold together when their Insights reach 0.65 similarity, or their subjects (“label. definition”) reach 0.75. The subject bar is higher because a shared word inflates the score. A Node with no Insights yet has only its name to compare, so it merges only on the subject bar. The pair that clears its bar by the widest margin merges first, and the larger Node keeps its name and place.
  • 5Name. Gemma names each automatic Node Concept with the nearest broader concept, in one to five words. A name that repeats a member’s title, wraps it (“About…”, “Study of…”), or is a catch-all like “Philosophy” or “Ideas” is rejected, and the model gets one corrective retry.
  • 6Grow. Each Node Concept hangs from exactly one parent: the Node whose subject is most similar to its own, chosen once and then kept. A Make Node, Branch, or Midpoint concept hangs from its source. The tree grows from its most central Node, and a new Node is placed off its parent in a direction where its line crosses no other line and stays clear of other Nodes, so adding a concept never rearranges the ones already there.
  • 7Connect. A line between two Node Concepts is (360 + 320·d) × 1.4 points long, where d is their distance, so more related subjects sit closer. An Insight’s connector is 150 + 180·n points long, where n is its distance to the Node’s centroid rescaled from 0 to 1 within that Node, so the closest member always sits nearest.

From distances to a tree

Meaning lives in 384 dimensions, but a screen shows two. Squeezing every pairwise distance onto the page can’t be done faithfully, and it tangles the lines. Instead, Angrove keeps the single most meaningful relationship for each concept: the subject closest to it. Those links form a tree, which can always be drawn without crossings, and each line’s length still reflects a real semantic distance.

Keeping the map steady

Parents and positions are saved, so a new concept joins the existing tree instead of setting off a fresh layout, and what you’ve already seen stays where it was. If two lines ever end up crossing, the smaller branch turns around the Node it hangs from until they clear; turning keeps every line’s length. A Midpoint can join concepts from different branches, so its lines to its other sources are drawn fainter, letting the tree read first.

Finding a Midpoint

Midpoint helps you find the idea that sits between two or more ideas you already have. It’s in the Insight Tree: select a few Insights and Angrove proposes a concept that connects them. It’s a good way to discover how two ideas relate when you can’t quite put it into words.

Try It

Watch it select an Insight from the Greek Philosophy tree and one from a new Node Concept, then open Midpoint. Drag on the tree, or use the percentages, to shift the balance. To try your own, tap Reset, then Select, choose two Insights, and tap Midpoint. After you Place a Midpoint, tap How It Works to watch the tree turn into the vectors behind it.

The computation

How It Works, above, draws the idea in two dimensions. This is what the app actually computes when you tap Place.

  • 1Reuse embeddings. Saved Insights already have stored embedding vectors. Midpoint reuses them when they came from the same model version; it does not need to regenerate them every time. When an embedding is needed, the source’s title and definition are joined, split into WordPiece tokens (up to 128), and run through all-MiniLM-L6-v2, a six-layer transformer on device through Core ML. Its token outputs are mean-pooled into one 384-dimensional vector v.
  • 2Normalize sources. Divide each source vector by its magnitude to get a unit vector âᵢ. This keeps its direction and gives every source magnitude 1, so your weights control its influence in the blend.
  • 3Weight. Your percentages become wᵢ = pᵢ / Σp, with negatives clipped to zero, across two to eight sources.
  • 4Blend and normalize again. Add the weighted unit vectors to form s, the weighted centroid. Divide this blend by its magnitude to get the target unit vector ĉ, preserving its new semantic direction.
  • 5Generate. Gemma 4 E4B, on device, receives each source’s title, definition, and weight, and returns five candidate concepts as JSON.
  • 6Validate and select. Each candidate’s title and definition are embedded the same way and normalized, giving êⱼ. Angrove compares them with the target and keeps the one with the smallest cosine distance. Its concept and definition are presented to you. If any embedding fails, it falls back to the model’s first candidate.

The first expression builds the target direction; the second chooses the candidate closest to it. Here Σ means “add across the sources,” and j* is the selected candidate’s index.

ĉ = Σ wᵢâᵢ / ‖Σ wᵢâᵢ‖     j* = argminj (1 − ĉ · êⱼ)

Why normalize before blending?

Normalization changes a vector’s magnitude without changing its direction. In two dimensions, arrows of magnitude 1 end on the unit circle. In three dimensions, their endpoints lie on the surface of the unit sphere. The same idea extends to the app’s 384-dimensional embedding space.

âᵢ = vᵢ / ‖vᵢ‖     ‖âᵢ‖ = 1

Dividing by the arrow’s length makes it a unit vector. This matters because vector addition and averaging are affected by length: a longer source vector would otherwise pull the result more strongly. Normalizing first ensures that your chosen weights, rather than arbitrary differences in embedding magnitude, determine each concept’s influence. Cosine similarity already ignores magnitude; it is the interpolation here that makes source normalization important.

A weighted semantic direction

Midpoint calculates a weighted centroid, or weighted vector blend, of the normalized sources. If A and B are unit vectors, 50/50 gives each equal influence; 70/30 pulls the blend toward A:

s₅₀/₅₀ = 0.5A + 0.5B

s₇₀/₃₀ = 0.7A + 0.3B

For several selected concepts, the same idea becomes s = Σ wᵢâᵢ: multiply each source by its share, then add the vectors. The nonnegative weights sum to 1 and represent how much influence each source has. The blend’s direction is the semantic target for the new concept.

Nlerp: the chord, then the sphere

This is normalized linear interpolation, or nlerp. For two sources, picture four steps: normalize their vectors so the endpoints lie on the unit sphere; draw the straight line segment, or chord, connecting the endpoints; select the weighted point along that chord; then normalize the result back to magnitude 1.

The chord usually passes through the sphere’s interior, not its exact center. It passes through the center only when the two source vectors point in exactly opposite directions. At 50/50 in that special case, the blend is zero and has no direction to normalize; the usual nlerp construction assumes a nonzero blend.

Why normalize the result again?

Even when both sources have magnitude 1, their linear blend usually does not. The endpoints lie on the sphere’s surface, but most points on the chord between distinct endpoints lie inside it. The blended vector therefore usually has magnitude less than 1. Normalizing again extends that arrow back to the surface while preserving the direction you just calculated:

ĉ = s / ‖s‖     ‖ĉ‖ = 1

This does not undo the interpolation: the new direction stays the same; only its length changes. Before normalization, the blend’s length reflects how much the sources agree. Identical sources give ‖s‖ = 1; two sources at angle φ, weighted 50/50, give ‖s‖ = cos(φ/2).

If the next operation were only a full cosine-similarity calculation, explicitly normalizing a nonzero result would not be strictly necessary: cosine already divides out magnitude. Here, normalization keeps the representation consistent and the interpolation explicitly in unit-vector space. Unit vectors are the canonical representation for this semantic geometry; the original raw embedding magnitudes are not restored.

Nlerp versus slerp

Nlerp uses the chord; slerp uses the arc. Spherical linear interpolation (slerp) follows the curved great-circle path on the unit sphere. It uses additional angular and trigonometric calculations so equal changes in the interpolation weight produce equal angular travel. Nlerp takes a linear weighted blend and then normalizes it, making it simpler and computationally cheaper.

These methods have different properties. Midpoint needs a useful semantic direction to compare with generated candidate embeddings; it does not require exact constant-speed angular travel. Nlerp provides that behavior with less complexity and extends naturally to several sources.

For two unit vectors that are not exactly opposite, nlerp and slerp give the same direction at 50/50. At biased weights such as 70/30, their directions can differ. In the figure, 30% of the way from â₁ to â₂ gives a direction at 27.0° rather than slerp’s 28°; the source directions are at 10° and 70°.

Cosine is a dot product here

Because ĉ and every candidate vector êⱼ have magnitude 1, cos θ = ĉ · êⱼ. The denominator in the full cosine formula is therefore 1, so a dot product is enough. Minimizing 1 − cos θ chooses the candidate with the smallest angle to the target: the closest semantic direction among the proposals.

Why the model writes first

An embedding vector cannot simply be decoded directly into a clean human-readable concept: MiniLM supplies a numerical representation, not a language decoder. Once the normalized weighted target is calculated, the language model proposes plausible concepts and definitions. Angrove embeds those candidates, compares their directions with the target, and presents the closest candidate and its definition.

The responsibilities are separate: the LLM generates plausible language and definitions; the embedding model represents semantic meaning numerically; deterministic code handles normalization, weighting, similarity comparison, and selection. For the Insight Tree, deterministic code also calculates and aligns the layout. The generated wording can vary, while the vector math checks which available proposal best fits the requested semantic position.

Organizing Your Work

As your conversations add up, two places help you keep them in order. Conversations in the menu lists everything you’ve discussed, and Study Topics lets you group conversations about the same subject, like chapters of a notebook.

What it does

Conversations helps you find and tidy past discussions. Study Topics collects related conversations, and their saved Insights, in one place. Each Study Topic also has its own Insight Tree that gathers every Insight you saved in any of its conversations, clustered and laid out just like a conversation’s tree, so you can see a whole subject across many discussions.

How to use it

Open Conversations to search, rename, pin, move to a Study Topic, or delete a conversation. Open Study Topics to create a topic, give it a short description, and add conversations to it. Inside a topic, tap Insight Tree at the top right, or swipe left, to open its tree; swipe right to return.

When it helps

Create a Study Topic called “Ethics” and collect conversations about virtue, conscience, and responsibility; its Insight Tree then shows how the terms you saved in each one connect. Pin a discussion you want to come back to soon.

Using the Library

The Library holds the original texts Angrove draws on when it answers, like Scripture and the writings of the Church Fathers. Open Library from the menu to read them yourself. It’s separate from your Insights, which are the definitions you saved.

What it does

The Library lets you read the sources behind Angrove’s answers in their own words.

How to use it

Open Library from the menu. Browse the works or search by title or subject, then open a work to read it. The Passage of the Day is an easy place to start.

When it helps

Read a source passage alongside a discussion to check the author’s own wording and context.

Finding the right passage

The Library holds 51,836 passages from 37 works, averaging about 970 characters each. Every passage was embedded ahead of time with the same MiniLM model, and the vectors ship with the app as a 79.6 MB file of 384-number rows that is memory-mapped rather than loaded. When you ask a question, Angrove embeds it and takes its dot product with every row, using Apple’s Accelerate library. Because the vectors are normalized, that dot product is the cosine similarity. Scanning all of them takes less time than writing the answer, so no approximate index is needed.

Layers before search

  • 1Citations. A named chapter like “John 14” is a lookup, not a search: the corpus is indexed by its own chapter tags, and one passage is taken from each cited chapter before a second from any.
  • 2Known sections. Questions that name a known section, or ask what a term means, go first to that section or to the Summa article that defines the term.
  • 3Named works. When a question names a work, such as the Didache, ranking happens inside that work, and passages that contain the question’s key terms rank first.
  • 4Search. Any slots still open, of the three references an answer can carry, go to semantic search across the whole Library. It ranks four candidates per open slot, because several chunks of one Summa article count only once and are replaced by Aquinas’s own answer from that article.

Two cutoffs, not one

A passage from semantic search is used only if its distance from your question is small enough. One cutoff can’t do both jobs: a bar loose enough to find the Good Samaritan also admits Livy on the Greek city of Nicaea when you ask about the Council of Nicaea, at 0.446. So the bar depends on what earlier layers found. Searching on its own, a passage needs a distance of 0.45 or less. Next to a citation or known section that already answered, extra passages must reach 0.38, so weak matches can’t pad a confident answer.

How 0.45 was chosen

The cutoff was swept against 56 test questions, including ones the Library shouldn’t answer at all:

CutoffCorrectNo passage foundNote
0.3861%17Tuned on doctrinal questions only; missed the Lord’s Prayer and the prodigal son
0.4575%7Still screens every out-of-scope question
0.50Higher—Starts grounding “how do I bake sourdough bread”

When nothing clears the bar, Angrove answers without sources. That is the safer failure: an answer with no citation is better than one grounded in the wrong text.

Home & Question of the Day

Home is the first page you see. Open it anytime with Home in the menu. It gathers ideas worth coming back to from your recent conversations, including a daily question to think about, so you can pick up where you left off.

What it does

The Question of the Day invites you to reflect on something unresolved from your recent conversations. Other cards appear when there’s something worth revisiting.

How to use it

Answer the Question of the Day to start a conversation about it; a new one appears later. Today in History offers a historical prompt, Loose Thread points to a subject in your tree that hasn’t connected to anything yet, Terms You Glossed Over shows a definition you looked up but never saved, and Quote From You shows something you wrote that stood out. Set a daily reminder in Settings → Notifications.

When it helps

Tap Loose Thread to jump straight to that subject in its tree, or save a glossed-over term from its card. It’s normal for Home to show only a few cards when you’re just starting out.

Model Tasks

Angrove runs entirely on your phone, so it works on one thing at a time: answering a question, defining a word, updating your tree. Anything else you ask for waits in line. Model Tasks shows that line, so you can see what Angrove is doing and change what happens next. Open it by tapping the status button at the bottom of a conversation, which reads Idle or Thinking.

Try It

Privacy & Your Data

Angrove answers on your phone without sending your questions to a model server. Your saved personal data is fully encrypted on your device. Settings → Privacy & Data is where you lock the app and back up or clear your data.

What it does

Fully Encrypted. Your saved personal data is encrypted with AES-256-GCM before it is stored, including conversations, attachments, saved Insights, Study Topics, personal settings, and local backups. Apple Keychain protects the encryption keys. Encryption is automatic; you do not need to turn it on or manage a password. The model runs on your phone too.

How to use it

Turn on App Lock to require Face ID, and choose how soon it locks. Export Conversations saves a readable JSON file, and Import Conversations reads one back. Exports are not encrypted; anyone with an exported file can read it. Clear Insight Tree removes saved Insights and tree data but keeps your conversations.

When it helps

Export before importing: importing replaces your current conversations. An export holds conversations only, not Insights or settings, and it contains readable discussion text, so keep it somewhere safe. To restore encrypted app data on another phone, restore both the app data and its Keychain through an encrypted device backup or a supported transfer that preserves both. Angrove cannot recover content if its encryption key is lost.

What encryption covers

Encryption protects saved personal data inside Angrove. The app decrypts content in memory while you use it. Readable exports, copied text, screenshots, notification previews, and the optional Lock Screen widget display are outside that storage boundary. Apple manages device-backup protection; this is not a claim of end-to-end encryption for iCloud Backup. The upgrade does not change older backups or exports.
